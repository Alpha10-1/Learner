## Why this matters at Tharisa

Requests often arrive as "can we use AI for this?" when the real need is a report (analytics) or a rule that runs by itself (automation). Naming the right tool sets the right expectations. Analytics and automation follow logic that people wrote and can test line by line. AI learns from data or generates new content, so its output has to be validated and reviewed before anyone acts on it.

## Learn

Three families of tools answer three different kinds of request.

**Analytics answers questions about data.** Microsoft's data-analyst training describes five types, each tied to one business question:

- *Descriptive*: what happened? (tonnes per shift last month)
- *Diagnostic*: why did it happen? (which factors line up with the low shifts)
- *Predictive*: what is likely to happen next? (a forecast from historical patterns)
- *Prescriptive*: what should we do? ("if we do this versus that, what's the likely result?")
- *Cognitive*: AI processing data at a scale people can't, such as sorting thousands of free-text comments by theme.

A person still decides what the numbers mean. The analyst's job is to pick the right type and to check that the data is trustworthy enough to act on.

**Automation runs the same steps every time something happens.** A Power Automate cloud flow, for example, starts on an event (an email arrives), on a button press, or on a schedule (a daily upload), then runs the actions someone configured. A person wrote the logic, so the same input gives the same output, and when it breaks you can find the faulty rule.

**AI produces outputs it learned from data rather than rules someone wrote**: predictions, classifications, generated text or images. That makes it useful for messy inputs, and it is also why it behaves differently from ordinary software. NIST's AI Risk Management Framework spells out how: AI depends on training data that may not fit the situation, its performance can drift as data or conditions change, it is harder to test and explain, and it can fail in ways nobody anticipated. Treat AI output as a draft or an estimate until someone has validated it.

**The lines blur, so name the part, not the product.** A dashboard is analytics; a forecast line inside it is predictive analytics; if a trained model produces that forecast, that part is AI. A flow that emails a supervisor when a value crosses a threshold is automation; if the flow first asks a language model to summarise operator comments, that step is AI. Each part needs its own kind of check.

### Worked example: one downtime request, three tools

A plant manager asks, "Can AI help us with conveyor downtime?" Break the request apart:

1. "How many hours did we lose to conveyor stops last month, by cause?" is **descriptive analytics**: a query and a chart. Check that the totals reconcile with the source system.
2. "Tell the shift supervisor when a conveyor stop has been open for more than two hours" is **automation**: a rule on an event or schedule. Test it with a stop open for 1 h 59 min and another open for 2 h 01 min.
3. "Which conveyors are likely to stop next week?" is **predictive analytics built with AI** (a trained model). Compare its predictions with what actually happened, and keep a person deciding what to do about them.

Only the third part needs AI, and it is the part that needs the most validation. Saying so early saves weeks and builds trust.

## Do

Sandbox: a blank page (paper, Notepad or OneNote). Allow 10 minutes.

1. Write three column headings: **Analytics**, **Automation**, **AI**.
2. Place each request below in a column. Some requests split across two columns; write down which part goes where.
    - a. "Show weekly plant throughput against plan for the last quarter."
    - b. "Send me a Teams message every morning at 06:00 with yesterday's totals."
    - c. "Summarise 400 free-text shift handover notes into the five most common issues."
    - d. "Why was recovery lower in September than in August?"
    - e. "Estimate next month's tonnage from the last two years of data."
    - f. "When a weighbridge ticket is missing a truck ID, put it on a review list."
3. For every item you placed under AI, write one sentence on how you would check its output before using it.
4. Compare your page with the expected result.

**Expected result:** a is analytics (descriptive). b is automation (a scheduled flow); the totals it sends come from an analytics query. c is AI: a language model summarising unstructured text, which Microsoft's list calls cognitive analytics. d is analytics (diagnostic). e is analytics (predictive), and it is AI if a trained model produces the estimate rather than a simple trend line. f is automation (a rule: missing value, then review list). Your checks for c and e compare the output with something known, such as reading a sample of the notes yourself or testing the estimate against months that have already happened.

## Check

**Q:** A rule emails a supervisor when a value crosses a threshold. Is that analytics, automation or AI, and why?

**A:** Automation. A person wrote the rule, it runs by itself when the event happens, and the same input always gives the same result.

**Q:** Name two ways AI behaves differently from rule-based software, according to the NIST AI RMF.

**A:** Any two of: it depends on training data that may not fit the situation; its performance can drift over time; it is harder to test and explain; it can fail in ways nobody anticipated; gathering large amounts of data raises privacy risk.

**Q:** A dashboard shows a forecast line for next month's tonnage. Which type of analytics is it, and what should you do before treating it as a target?

**A:** Predictive analytics. It is an estimate from historical patterns, so check how well the method would have predicted months you already know, and label it clearly as a forecast rather than a fact.
